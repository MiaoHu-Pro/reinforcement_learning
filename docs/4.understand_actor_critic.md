# Understanding Actor–Critic

This note explains Actor–Critic theory and the implementation in
[`code-6-actor-critic.py`](../rl_learning_demo/day02/code-6-actor-critic.py).

The example uses an **n-step Actor–Critic** algorithm on CartPole-v1. It learns
from short rollouts of at most 20 transitions instead of waiting for the entire
episode.

## 1. What is Actor–Critic?

Actor–Critic combines two learners:

```text
Actor
    learns what action to take
    represents policy pi_theta(a|s)

Critic
    learns how good a state is
    represents value V_phi(s)
```

The actor produces an action distribution. The critic evaluates the states
visited by the actor and supplies a learning signal called a temporal-
difference error or advantage estimate.

```text
State S_t
   │
   ├──► Actor pi_theta(.|S_t) ──► sample action A_t
   │                                  │
   │                                  ▼
   │                             Environment
   │                                  │
   │                                  ▼
   │                         reward R_t, state S_(t+1)
   │
   └──► Critic V_phi(S_t) ◄──── evaluates the transition
```

The word **actor** comes from taking actions. The word **critic** comes from
judging whether the outcome was better or worse than expected.

## 2. Actor network

The actor represents a stochastic policy:

$$
\pi_\theta(a|s)
= P(A_t=a\mid S_t=s)
$$

In the code, `ActorNet` maps a four-dimensional CartPole state to two logits:

```text
cart position       ┐
cart velocity       │
pole angle          ├─► 128 hidden units ─► left/right logits
pole angular speed  ┘
```

The logits define a categorical action distribution:

```python
distribution = Categorical(logits=self.actor(state_tensor))
```

During training, the action is sampled to provide exploration:

```python
action = distribution.sample()
```

During evaluation, the highest-logit action is selected:

```python
action = torch.argmax(logits)
```

## 3. Critic network

The critic approximates the state-value function:

$$
V^\pi(s)
= \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s]
$$

The neural-network approximation is:

$$
V_\phi(s) \approx V^\pi(s)
$$

`CriticNet` maps the same four-dimensional state to one scalar:

```text
state ─► 128 hidden units ─► one predicted value V_phi(s)
```

The critic does not select actions. Its output estimates future discounted
return under the current actor policy.

## 4. From REINFORCE to Actor–Critic

### `code-4-reinforce-policy-gradient-step-by-step.py`: plain REINFORCE

The policy is weighted by a complete sampled reward-to-go:

$$
G_t
= R_t+\gamma R_{t+1}+\gamma^2R_{t+2}+\cdots
$$

The complete episode must finish before all `G_t` values are known.

### `code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py`: REINFORCE with a value baseline

The policy uses the Monte Carlo advantage:

$$
Adv_t
= G_t-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

It still waits for the entire episode because `G_t` contains all observed
rewards through the final step.

### `code-6-actor-critic.py`: n-step Actor–Critic

The critic supplies a bootstrapped estimate before the episode finishes:

$$
G_t^{(n)}
= R_t+\gamma R_{t+1}+\cdots
+\gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n-1}
+\gamma^nV_\phi(S_{t+n})
$$

The actor uses:

$$
Adv_t^{(n)}
= G_t^{(n)}-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

Because the final unknown part is approximated by the critic, the networks can
update after a short rollout rather than waiting for episode termination.

## 5. One-step TD error

The simplest Actor–Critic uses one transition:

$$
\delta_t
= R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

This is the one-step temporal-difference error. It can be interpreted as a
one-step advantage estimate:

```text
delta_t > 0
    outcome was better than the critic predicted
    → increase probability of A_t

delta_t < 0
    outcome was worse than predicted
    → decrease probability of A_t
```

### What do the three terms mean?

$$
\delta_t
= \underbrace{R_t}_{\text{reward now}}
+\underbrace{\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})}_{\text{estimated future value}}
-\underbrace{V_\phi(S_t)}_{\text{value predicted before acting}}
$$

The critic first predicted `V_phi(S_t)`. After action `A_t` is executed, the
agent observes the immediate reward and next state. It forms a revised one-step
estimate of what the old state was worth:

$$
y_t^{TD}=R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})
$$

The TD error is simply the difference between this new target and the old
prediction:

$$
\delta_t=y_t^{TD}-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

It is called **temporal-difference** learning because it compares value
predictions made at two consecutive times: `t` and `t+1`.

### Why does the critic use TD error?

The critic wants its current prediction to move toward the one-step target:

$$
V_\phi(S_t)
\longrightarrow
R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})
$$

The critic can minimize a loss such as:

$$
L_{critic}=\delta_t^2
$$

or the Huber version used by the examples. If `delta_t` is positive, the
critic's old value was too low. If `delta_t` is negative, its old value was too
high.

The key benefit is that the critic does not need to wait for the final episode
return. It learns from the reward and next state as soon as a transition is
available.

### Why is TD error an advantage estimate?

The true advantage function compares the value of taking a particular action
with the average value of being in that state:

$$
A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)
$$

The action-value function satisfies the one-step Bellman relationship:

$$
Q^\pi(S_t,A_t)
=\mathbb{E}_\pi
\left[R_t+\gamma V^\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t,A_t\right]
$$

After one sampled transition, the quantity

$$
R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})
$$

is a sample-based approximation of `Q^pi(S_t,A_t)`. Subtracting the critic's
state value gives:

$$
R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})-V_\phi(S_t)
\approx Q^\pi(S_t,A_t)-V^\pi(S_t)
=A^\pi(S_t,A_t)
$$

Therefore:

$$
\delta_t\approx A^\pi(S_t,A_t)
$$

This approximation becomes more accurate as the critic becomes a better
estimate of the true state-value function. Early in training, the critic is
inaccurate, so `delta_t` is a noisy and biased advantage estimate.

### Why does the actor need an advantage rather than only reward?

An immediate reward does not tell the actor whether the selected action was
better than what was normally expected in that state. The advantage supplies a
state-dependent comparison:

```text
raw reward:
    "I received 1."

advantage/TD error:
    "After accounting for the next state, this result was better or worse
     than the critic expected from S_t."
```

The actor loss uses:

$$
L_{actor}
=-\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)\operatorname{stopgrad}(\delta_t)
$$

- Positive `delta_t`: increase the probability of the sampled action.
- Negative `delta_t`: decrease the probability of the sampled action.
- Near-zero `delta_t`: make only a small policy update.

The critic's value acts as a baseline. This removes much of the variation caused
by some states naturally having larger future returns than other states.

### Numerical examples

Assume `gamma = 0.98`.

#### Better than expected

```text
V(S_t)     = 10
R_t        = 1
V(S_(t+1)) = 12
```

$$
\delta_t=1+0.98(12)-10=2.76
$$

The transition was better than expected, so the actor increases the
probability of `A_t`.

#### Worse than expected

```text
V(S_t)     = 10
R_t        = 1
V(S_(t+1)) = 7
```

$$
\delta_t=1+0.98(7)-10=-2.14
$$

The transition was worse than expected, so the actor decreases the probability
of `A_t`.

#### True terminal transition

At a true terminal state there is no future task value, so the bootstrap is
zero:

$$
\delta_t=R_t-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

If `V(S_t)=10` and the final reward is `1`:

$$
\delta_t=1-10=-9
$$

The critic strongly overestimated that state/action outcome.

### Why is one-step TD error important?

One-step TD error is important because it connects all parts of Actor–Critic:

1. **It trains the critic.** It measures value-prediction error.
2. **It trains the actor.** It provides a local advantage estimate.
3. **It enables bootstrapping.** Learning can occur without waiting for the
   complete episode return.
4. **It reduces variance.** The critic replaces the unknown distant future
   with a learned estimate.
5. **It supports frequent updates.** Online Actor–Critic can update after each
   transition.
6. **It links value learning and policy learning.** The critic evaluates the
   actor's experience, and the actor changes behavior using that evaluation.

The trade-off is bias: if `V_phi` is inaccurate, both the TD target and
advantage estimate inherit that error. This is why critic stability is central
to Actor–Critic methods.

`code-6-actor-critic.py` generalizes this idea to 20-step rollouts. Longer
rollouts usually reduce bootstrap bias but increase sampling variance.

### Explicit one-step TD-error example

[`code-7-actor-critic-one-step-td-error.py`](../rl_learning_demo/day02/code-7-actor-critic-one-step-td-error.py)
stores every `(S_t, A_t, R_t, S_(t+1))` transition and explicitly calculates:

$$
\delta_t
= R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

Its critic always trains directly from this one-step TD target. By default, the
actor combines the TD errors using generalized advantage estimation:

$$
A_t^{GAE}
= \delta_t+\gamma\lambda\delta_{t+1}
+(\gamma\lambda)^2\delta_{t+2}+\cdots
$$

With `--gae-lambda 0`, this reduces exactly to:

$$
A_t^{GAE}=\delta_t
$$

The default `--gae-lambda 0.95` gives more stable multi-step credit assignment
while remaining constructed entirely from the explicit one-step TD errors.

## 6. Bias–variance trade-off

Monte Carlo and temporal-difference learning occupy different ends of a
spectrum:

| Target | Bootstrap? | Wait for episode? | Bias | Variance |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| full `G_t` | no | yes | lower | higher |
| one-step TD | strongly | no | higher | lower |
| n-step return | partly | not normally | middle | middle |

Bootstrapping uses an estimate `V_phi(S_(t+n))` inside the learning target. If
the critic is inaccurate, the target inherits some of that approximation
error. This introduces bias but often reduces variance enough to improve
sample efficiency.

The rollout length is configurable:

```bash
--rollout-steps 20
```

Special cases:

```text
n = 1
    one-step Actor–Critic

n = full remaining episode length
    approaches Monte Carlo REINFORCE with a value baseline
```

## 7. Training timeline

Suppose one episode lasts 55 steps and `rollout_steps=20`:

```text
Episode begins
    │
    ├─ collect steps  0–19  → update actor and critic
    ├─ collect steps 20–39  → update actor and critic
    └─ collect steps 40–54  → update actor and critic
Episode ends
```

Therefore:

- one episode still contains one complete trajectory;
- the trajectory is processed in several short rollouts;
- one episode can produce several actor/critic updates;
- policy weights can change before the episode finishes.

This differs from `code-4-reinforce-policy-gradient-step-by-step.py` and `code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py`:

```text
REINFORCE:
    one complete episode → one update

n-step Actor–Critic:
    one short rollout → one update
    one episode may contain several rollouts and updates
```

## 8. Collecting one rollout

The training loop creates fresh rollout lists:

```python
rollout_states = []
rollout_actions = []
rollout_rewards = []
```

It collects at most `rollout_steps` transitions:

```python
for _ in range(rollout_steps):
    action = agent.choose_action(state)
    next_state, reward, terminated, truncated, _ = env.step(action)

    rollout_states.append(state)
    rollout_actions.append(action)
    rollout_rewards.append(float(reward))
    state = next_state

    if terminated or truncated:
        break
```

The rollout may be shorter than 20 steps if the episode ends.

## 9. Why collection uses `torch.no_grad()`

Action selection contains:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    logits = self.actor(state_tensor)
```

The environment is not differentiable. Retaining an autograd graph while
CartPole executes would waste memory. The rollout stores ordinary NumPy states
and integer actions. `update()` later evaluates all stored states again with
gradient tracking enabled.

This is valid because no policy update occurs during the short rollout. The
stored actions were sampled from the same policy parameters used when their
log-probabilities are reconstructed.

## 10. Bootstrapping at the rollout boundary

If the rollout stops after `n` transitions but the episode is still running,
the future return is unknown. The critic estimates it:

```python
bootstrap_value = agent.estimate_value(state)
```

The return calculation starts from this estimate and works backward:

```python
return_from_t = bootstrap_value

for reward in reversed(rewards):
    return_from_t = reward + gamma * return_from_t
```

For a three-step rollout:

$$
G_{t+2}^{(3)}
= R_{t+2}+\gamma V(S_{t+3})
$$

$$
G_{t+1}^{(3)}
= R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2V(S_{t+3})
$$

$$
G_t^{(3)}
= R_t+\gamma R_{t+1}+\gamma^2R_{t+2}
+\gamma^3V(S_{t+3})
$$

## 11. Terminal state versus truncation

Gymnasium returns both:

```python
terminated
truncated
```

At a true terminal state, future task value is zero:

```python
bootstrap_value = 0.0 if terminated else agent.estimate_value(state)
```

At a time-limit truncation, the task did not naturally terminate. The code
therefore retains the critic bootstrap value. This distinction prevents a time
limit from being incorrectly treated as a zero-value terminal state.

## 12. Batched update tensors

For a rollout containing `m` transitions:

| Tensor | Shape | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| `state_tensor` | `[m, 4]` | rollout states |
| `action_tensor` | `[m]` | sampled actions |
| actor logits | `[m, 2]` | left/right action scores |
| log probabilities | `[m]` | `log pi(A_t|S_t)` |
| `value_estimates` | `[m]` | critic predictions |
| `returns` | `[m]` | n-step bootstrapped targets |
| `advantages` | `[m]` | return minus value estimate |

The update reconstructs the actor distribution and critic values:

```python
distribution = Categorical(logits=self.actor(state_tensor))
selected_log_probabilities = distribution.log_prob(action_tensor)
value_estimates = self.critic(state_tensor)
advantages = returns - value_estimates
```

## 13. Actor loss

Ignoring the optional entropy term for a moment, the actor loss is:

$$
L_{actor}
= -\frac{1}{m}\sum_t
\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)
\operatorname{stopgrad}(Adv_t^{(n)})
$$

In code:

```python
actor_loss = -(
    selected_log_probabilities * advantages.detach()
).mean()
```

The advantage controls the direction and strength of the policy update.

### Why `detach()` is essential

`advantages` contains `value_estimates`, which are connected to `CriticNet`.
The actor must treat the critic's assessment as a fixed learning signal:

```python
advantages.detach()
```

Without `detach()`, `actor_loss.backward()` could train the critic merely to
make the actor loss smaller. The critic should instead be trained only by its
own value-prediction loss.

## 14. Entropy bonus

The code includes a small entropy bonus:

```python
entropy = distribution.entropy().mean()

actor_loss = policy_gradient_loss - entropy_coefficient * entropy
```

The complete actor objective is:

$$
L_{actor}
= -\frac{1}{m}\sum_t
\log\pi_\theta(A_t|S_t)
\operatorname{stopgrad}(Adv_t^{(n)})
-\beta\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|S_t))
$$

Entropy is high when action probabilities are less concentrated. Subtracting a
small entropy term discourages the actor from becoming deterministic too early
and losing exploration. The example uses:

```python
entropy_coefficient = 0.001
```

## 15. Critic loss

The critic learns to match the n-step return:

$$
V_\phi(S_t) \approx G_t^{(n)}
$$

The code uses Huber loss:

```python
critic_loss = F.smooth_l1_loss(value_estimates, returns)
```

Huber loss behaves approximately like squared error for small errors and like
absolute error for large errors. It is less sensitive than MSE to occasional
large, moving bootstrapped targets.

The critic's target is treated as fixed because the bootstrap value was
calculated inside `torch.no_grad()`.

## 16. Backward and optimizer workflow

```python
self.actor_optimizer.zero_grad()
self.critic_optimizer.zero_grad()

actor_loss.backward()
critic_loss.backward()

self.actor_optimizer.step()
self.critic_optimizer.step()
```

The operations mean:

```text
actor_loss.backward()
    → computes gradients for actor parameters theta

critic_loss.backward()
    → computes gradients for critic parameters phi

actor_optimizer.step()
    → changes theta

critic_optimizer.step()
    → changes phi
```

As before, `backward()` calculates gradients but does not change weights.
`optimizer.step()` performs the actual update.

Before stepping, gradients are clipped:

```python
clip_grad_norm_(parameters, max_norm=1.0)
```

Gradient clipping limits unusually large updates and improves stability when
the critic target changes during training.

## 17. Full update flow

```text
Collect up to n transitions
            │
            ▼
Estimate V(S_end) for bootstrapping
            │
            ▼
Calculate n-step returns backward
            │
            ├───────────────┐
            ▼               ▼
Actor logits           Critic values
log pi(A_t|S_t)        V(S_t)
            │               │
            └───────┬───────┘
                    ▼
          advantage = return - value
                    │
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          ▼                   ▼
 actor loss with         critic Huber loss
 detached advantage      against n-step return
          │                   │
          ▼                   ▼
 actor backward          critic backward
          │                   │
          ▼                   ▼
 update actor            update critic
```

## 18. Comparison of the three current algorithms

| Feature | `code-4-reinforce-policy-gradient-step-by-step.py` | `code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py` | `code-6-actor-critic.py` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Algorithm | REINFORCE | REINFORCE + baseline | n-step Actor–Critic |
| Policy/actor network | yes | yes | yes |
| Value/critic network | no | yes | yes |
| Return target | full Monte Carlo | full Monte Carlo | n-step bootstrap |
| Wait for episode end | yes | yes | no |
| Updates per episode | one | one actor + one value | potentially several |
| Advantage | `G_t` | `G_t-V(S_t)` | `G_t^(n)-V(S_t)` |
| Bootstrapping | no | no | yes |
| Typical bias | lower | lower policy-gradient bias | higher from bootstrap |
| Typical variance | higher | reduced by baseline | further controlled by n-step target |

## 19. Why `code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py` was called REINFORCE with baseline

`code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py` already has a policy and value network, so it looks actor–critic-
like. The more precise distinction is its update target:

```text
code-5-reinforce-policy-gradient-value-baseline.py
    waits for full episode
    critic target = complete Monte Carlo G_t

code-6-actor-critic.py
    updates from short rollouts
    critic target includes bootstrap V(S_(t+n))
```

In practice, terminology varies, but bootstrapped, repeated updates before the
episode ends are characteristic of Actor–Critic methods.

## 20. Understanding the training output

The program prints:

```text
total reward
mean actor loss
mean critic loss
mean advantage
```

Neither actor nor critic loss must decrease monotonically:

- the actor changes the state distribution;
- the critic's bootstrap targets move as the critic learns;
- longer episodes expose the networks to different states;
- the policy continues sampling actions during training.

Episode return and its moving average are the primary task-performance
measurements.

## 22. Summary

Actor–Critic divides reinforcement learning into two cooperating jobs:

$$
\text{Actor: improve }\pi_\theta(a|s)
$$

$$
\text{Critic: improve }V_\phi(s)
$$

The critic creates an n-step advantage estimate:

$$
Adv_t^{(n)}
= G_t^{(n)}-V_\phi(S_t)
$$

The actor uses that estimate to decide whether the sampled action should become
more or less probable. Because the critic bootstraps the unknown future, both
networks can learn from short rollouts before the full episode finishes.
