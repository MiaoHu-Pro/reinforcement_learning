# Understanding Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

This note explains the theory and implementation of:

- the legacy [`day04/code-1.py`](../rl_learning_demo/day04/code-1.py);
- the modern [`day04/code-1-grpo.py`](../rl_learning_demo/day04/code-1-grpo.py).

The modern program is a small CartPole adaptation of GRPO's central idea. It is
intended to make group-relative advantages and clipped policy optimization easy
to inspect. Full language-model GRPO systems additionally involve prompts,
token completions, reward models or verifiers, and often a separate reference
model with an explicit KL penalty.

## 1. The central GRPO idea

GRPO trains a policy by comparing a **group of outputs for the same task**.

For one task or prompt, sample $G$ outputs:

$$
o_1,o_2,\ldots,o_G.
$$

Give each output a reward:

$$
R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_G.
$$

Instead of training a value network to predict a baseline, GRPO constructs the
baseline from the group itself. A common group-relative advantage is:

$$
\boxed{
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{
R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)
}{
\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)+\epsilon
}
}
$$

Interpretation:

- $\widehat A_i>0$: output $i$ performed better than its group.
- $\widehat A_i<0$: output $i$ performed worse than its group.
- $\widehat A_i\approx0$: output $i$ was near the group average.

The policy increases probabilities of relatively successful outputs and
decreases probabilities of relatively unsuccessful outputs.

## 2. Why is it called group-relative?

The reward is interpreted relative to other samples for the same task.

Suppose three trajectories return:

$$
[10,20,30].
$$

Their mean is:

$$
\mu=20,
$$

and their population standard deviation is approximately:

$$
\sigma=8.165.
$$

The standardized group advantages are approximately:

$$
[-1.225,0,1.225].
$$

The trajectory with return 30 is encouraged, the trajectory with return 10 is
discouraged, and the average trajectory produces little policy signal.

The absolute reward scale is less important than the ordering and separation
inside the group.

## 3. What is the common task in CartPole?

In language-model GRPO, a group commonly contains multiple completions sampled
for the same prompt.

CartPole has no prompt, so the modern script uses the following analogy:

```text
one prompt/task        <--> one initial CartPole state
one completion         <--> one complete CartPole trajectory
completion reward      <--> episode return
completion tokens      <--> actions in the trajectory
```

Every trajectory in one modern training group calls `env.reset()` with the
same seed. They therefore begin from the same initial CartPole state. The
policy remains stochastic, so it samples different action sequences and
produces different returns.

This makes the comparison more controlled than using unrelated initial states.

## 4. GRPO removes the learned critic in this example

An Actor–Critic method contains:

- an actor $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$;
- a critic $V_\phi(s)$.

It may estimate advantage with:

$$
\delta_t
=
R_t+\gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1})-V_\phi(S_t),
$$

or with GAE:

$$
\widehat A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}}
=
\delta_t+\gamma\lambda\widehat A_{t+1}^{\mathrm{GAE}}.
$$

The CartPole GRPO script has no `ValueNet`, no value loss, and no critic
optimizer. Its group statistics replace the learned baseline:

$$
\text{learned baseline }V_\phi(S_t)
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{group mean reward }\overline R.
$$

This reduces model and optimizer complexity. However, it requires multiple
samples for every comparison group.

## 5. What reward does each CartPole trajectory receive?

CartPole produces reward 1 for every successful step. The undiscounted episode
return is therefore:

$$
R_i
=
\sum_{t=0}^{T_i-1}r_{i,t}
=
T_i.
$$

A longer-balanced trajectory receives a larger return.

The legacy script divides the episode return by 200:

```python
normalized_reward = episode_reward / 200.0
```

This division is unnecessary before group standardization. For any positive
constant $c$:

$$
\frac{cR_i-\operatorname{mean}(cR)}{\operatorname{std}(cR)}
=
\frac{R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R)}{\operatorname{std}(R)}.
$$

The positive scale factor cancels. The modern script therefore standardizes raw
episode returns directly.

## 6. One scalar advantage is assigned to a complete trajectory

The modern program calculates one group-relative advantage $\widehat A_i$ for
trajectory $i$. Every action in that trajectory uses the same advantage:

$$
\widehat A_{i,0}
=
\widehat A_{i,1}
=
\cdots
=
\widehat A_{i,T_i-1}
=
\widehat A_i.
$$

If a trajectory is better than the group average, all its sampled actions are
reinforced. If it is worse, all its sampled actions are discouraged.

This is outcome-level credit assignment. It is simpler but coarser than TD or
GAE, which supplies a different learning signal at every state-action pair.

## 7. Why must the old policy remain fixed during collection?

The policy that generates the group is called the behavior or old policy:

$$
\pi_{\mathrm{old}}.
$$

For every selected action, the script saves:

$$
\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t}).
$$

No `backward()` or optimizer `step()` occurs during group collection. Therefore
all group members are sampled from one unchanged policy version.

After collection, the old log probabilities become fixed data. The trainable
policy $\pi_\theta$ may then change across repeated optimization epochs.

## 8. The GRPO/PPO probability ratio

During optimization, the policy recalculates:

$$
\log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t}).
$$

The probability ratio is:

$$
\boxed{
r_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{
\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t})
}{
\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t})
}
}
$$

It is implemented in log space:

```python
log_ratios = new_log_probabilities - old_log_probabilities
probability_ratios = torch.exp(log_ratios)
```

because:

$$
\exp(\log x-\log y)=\frac{x}{y}.
$$

Interpretation:

- $r_{i,t}=1$: the action probability is unchanged.
- $r_{i,t}>1$: the new policy makes the action more likely.
- $r_{i,t}<1$: the new policy makes the action less likely.

## 9. The clipped group-relative objective

For each transition, the unclipped surrogate is:

$$
s_{i,t}^{(1)}
=
r_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i.
$$

With clipping distance $\varepsilon$, the clipped surrogate is:

$$
s_{i,t}^{(2)}
=
\operatorname{clip}
\left(
r_{i,t}(\theta),
1-\varepsilon,
1+\varepsilon
\right)
\widehat A_i.
$$

The conservative surrogate selects the smaller value:

$$
\min\left(s_{i,t}^{(1)},s_{i,t}^{(2)}\right).
$$

The modern script gives every trajectory equal total weight:

$$
\boxed{
L_{\mathrm{policy}}(\theta)
=
-\frac1G
\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac1{T_i}
\sum_{t=0}^{T_i-1}
\min\left(
r_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\operatorname{clip}
\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)
\widehat A_i
\right)
}
$$

The default is:

$$
\varepsilon=0.2,
$$

so ratios are clipped to:

$$
[0.8,1.2].
$$

## 10. Why use clipping?

The same collected group is reused for several optimizer steps. Without a
constraint, repeated updates could make the new policy very different from the
old policy that generated the data.

For a positive advantage, the algorithm wants to increase the action
probability. Once the ratio is larger than $1+\varepsilon$, the clipped branch
removes the incentive for further beneficial-looking growth.

For a negative advantage, the algorithm wants to decrease the action
probability. Once the ratio is smaller than $1-\varepsilon$, clipping removes
the incentive for further beneficial-looking reduction.

Clipping is a soft policy-movement constraint, not a guarantee that every ratio
will remain inside the interval.

## 11. Where is the log-policy gradient?

The GRPO loss does not visibly multiply
$\widehat A_i\log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t})$. Like PPO, it differentiates
through the ratio.

Because the old log probability is detached:

$$
\nabla_\theta r_{i,t}(\theta)
=
r_{i,t}(\theta)
\nabla_\theta
\log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t}).
$$

On an active unclipped branch:

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_\theta L_{i,t}
=
-r_{i,t}(\theta)
\widehat A_i
\nabla_\theta
\log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t})
}
$$

At the start of the first update, $r_{i,t}\approx1$, producing the familiar
REINFORCE-style direction:

$$
-\widehat A_i
\nabla_\theta
\log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t}).
$$

PyTorch constructs this gradient when `loss.backward()` traverses:

```text
loss
  -> clipped minimum
  -> probability ratio * group advantage
  -> exp(new log probability - old log probability)
  -> log pi_theta(action | state)
  -> policy parameters theta
```

## 12. Equal trajectory weighting matters

CartPole's better trajectories are longer, so a simple concatenated mean over
all transitions would automatically give successful trajectories more weight
because they contain more actions.

The legacy implementation first averages each trajectory's loss over time and
then averages trajectories:

$$
\frac1G\sum_i\frac1{T_i}\sum_t L_{i,t}.
$$

The modern script flattens variable-length trajectories for efficient tensor
processing but preserves the same weighting with:

$$
w_{i,t}=\frac1{T_i}.
$$

It computes:

$$
\frac1G\sum_i\sum_t w_{i,t}L_{i,t}.
$$

Thus every trajectory contributes equal total weight regardless of length.

## 13. Entropy regularization

The modern actor loss includes a small entropy bonus:

$$
L(\theta)
=
L_{\mathrm{policy}}(\theta) -
\alpha\mathcal H(\pi_\theta).
$$

Entropy is larger when action probabilities are less concentrated. Subtracting
the entropy bonus from a minimized loss discourages the stochastic policy from
becoming deterministic too early.

The default coefficient is small:

$$
\alpha=0.001.
$$

## 14. Approximate KL and early stopping

PPO-style clipping is not a hard distance limit. The modern script also
monitors the approximate KL contribution:

$$
\widehat D_{\mathrm{KL}}
=
(r-1)-\log r.
$$

The implementation averages this with the same equal-trajectory weighting used
by the policy loss.

If mean approximate KL exceeds `--target-kl`, it stops reusing the current
group early. The default is:

$$
\text{target KL}=0.02.
$$

This KL is a diagnostic between the changing policy and the behavior policy
that collected the current group.

## 15. This example does not use an explicit reference-model KL penalty

Full GRPO formulations often include a separate reference policy
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and a penalty such as:

$$
-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
\right).
$$

Its role is to discourage a trained language model from drifting too far from a
stable reference model.

The CartPole example has no pretrained reference model. It uses:

- stored behavior-policy log probabilities;
- ratio clipping;
- approximate-KL early stopping;
- gradient clipping.

Therefore, this script demonstrates group-relative clipped optimization, but
it should not be mistaken for a complete large-language-model GRPO training
stack.

## 16. Complete collection and update workflow

For training group $k$:

```text
choose one common initial-state seed
                |
                v
collect trajectory 1 with pi_old -> return R_1
collect trajectory 2 with pi_old -> return R_2
...
collect trajectory G with pi_old -> return R_G
                |
                v
calculate group mean and standard deviation
                |
                v
calculate one relative advantage A_i per trajectory
                |
                v
repeat clipped optimization epochs on this group
                |
                v
discard the group
                |
                v
collect a fresh group with the updated policy
```

There are no network updates during collection. There may be several policy
updates after the group is complete.

## 17. Implementation walkthrough

### Stage 1: collect one trajectory

`collect_trajectory()` stores:

```python
states
actions
old_log_probabilities
episode_return
```

It does not need to store rewards at every step because the modern example uses
only the final episode return for group comparison.

### Stage 2: collect the comparison group

`collect_group()` calls `collect_trajectory()` $G$ times with the same initial
seed and unchanged policy parameters.

### Stage 3: calculate group advantages

`calculate_group_advantages()` performs:

```python
returns = tensor([trajectory.episode_return for trajectory in trajectories])
advantages = (returns - returns.mean()) / (
    returns.std(unbiased=False) + 1e-8
)
```

`unbiased=False` selects the population standard deviation of the complete
sampled group, matching NumPy's default used in the legacy code.

### Stage 4: flatten variable-length trajectories

`prepare_group_batch()` concatenates all states, actions, old log probabilities,
and repeated advantages. It also creates a per-transition weight $1/T_i$.

### Stage 5: calculate the ratio and clipped objective

```python
new_log_probabilities = distribution.log_prob(actions)
log_ratios = new_log_probabilities - old_log_probabilities
probability_ratios = torch.exp(log_ratios)
clipped_ratios = torch.clamp(
    probability_ratios,
    1.0 - clip_epsilon,
    1.0 + clip_epsilon,
)
```

The two surrogate branches are:

```python
unclipped_surrogate = probability_ratios * repeated_advantages
clipped_surrogate = clipped_ratios * repeated_advantages
```

### Stage 6: calculate the equally weighted loss

```python
conservative_surrogate = torch.minimum(
    unclipped_surrogate,
    clipped_surrogate,
)

policy_loss = -(
    conservative_surrogate * trajectory_weights
).sum() / group_size
```

### Stage 7: backward and optimizer step

```python
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
clip_grad_norm_(policy.parameters(), max_norm=0.5)
optimizer.step()
```

- `zero_grad()` clears gradients from the preceding epoch.
- `backward()` calculates policy gradients.
- gradient clipping limits unusually large updates.
- `step()` changes the policy weights.

## 18. What happens if all group returns are equal?

If:

$$
R_1=R_2=\cdots=R_G,
$$

then:

$$
R_i-\overline R=0
$$

for every trajectory. Therefore:

$$
\widehat A_i=0.
$$

The clipped policy-gradient component becomes zero. Only the small entropy bonus
can produce a policy update in the modern example.

This is an important limitation of group-relative learning: a group needs
reward variation to express preferences.

## 19. Effect of group size

A small group is cheaper to sample but produces noisy estimates of group mean
and standard deviation. A larger group provides a more stable comparison but
requires more environment episodes per update.

The legacy example uses:

$$
G=5.
$$

The modern example defaults to:

$$
G=8.
$$

The modern script requires at least two trajectories because one sample cannot
meaningfully define relative group performance.

## 20. GRPO compared with earlier policy-gradient methods

| Method | Actor learning signal | Learned critic? | Samples before update | Data reuse |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| REINFORCE | Monte Carlo return | No | one trajectory | once |
| REINFORCE with baseline | return minus value | Yes | one trajectory | once |
| Actor–Critic | TD/GAE advantage | Yes | transition or rollout | usually limited |
| PPO | GAE advantage | Usually yes | rollout batch | several clipped epochs |
| This GRPO example | standardized group return | No | group of trajectories | several clipped epochs |

PPO and this GRPO example share the clipped probability-ratio mechanism. Their
main advantage-estimation difference is:

```text
PPO:
    critic values -> TD errors -> GAE per transition

GRPO example:
    group returns -> group mean/std -> one advantage per trajectory
```

## 22. Why the modern script has a finite training limit

The legacy training loop uses `while True` and stops only when its most recently
collected group exceeds a threshold. This can run indefinitely and bases the
decision on a noisy single group.

The modern script:

- limits training with `--max-groups`;
- averages recent group returns over `--solved-window` groups;
- stops when that recent mean reaches `--target-return`.

For `CartPole-v1`, the maximum episode return is 500, so the default target is:

$$
475.
$$

## 24. Important limitations of this teaching example

### Coarse credit assignment

Every action in one trajectory receives the same outcome-level advantage. The
method cannot directly identify which individual action caused success or
failure.

### Environment sampling cost

Removing the critic saves value-model training, but each update requires a group
of complete episodes.

### Group dependence

An output is labeled good or bad relative to its sampled group. A different
group can produce a different normalized advantage for the same absolute
return.

### No explicit reference model

The example constrains movement from the behavior policy for one update but
does not maintain a separate stable reference policy.

### CartPole is not the original GRPO use case

This adaptation demonstrates the mathematics cleanly. It does not reproduce
the infrastructure or sequence-level details of language-model post-training.

## 25. Final mental model

GRPO asks:

> For the same task, which sampled outputs were better or worse than the other
> outputs in this group?

The CartPole version answers this by:

```text
same initial state
       |
       v
several stochastic trajectories
       |
       v
episode returns
       |
       v
standardized group-relative advantages
       |
       v
clipped probability-ratio policy updates
```

The two central equations are:

$$
\boxed{
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{R_i-\overline R}{\sigma_R+\epsilon}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
L_{\mathrm{policy}}(\theta)
=
-\frac1G
\sum_i\frac1{T_i}\sum_t
\min\left(
r_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
\operatorname{clip}
\left(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)
\widehat A_i
\right)
}
$$

The group supplies the baseline, so no learned critic is required. The clipped
ratio permits controlled reuse of the collected group.
